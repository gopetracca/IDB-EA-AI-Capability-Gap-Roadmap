Capability Views Catalogue

Ten ways to put one capability assessment in front of a management audience — each showing what it answers, who it is for, and what data it needs before it can be produced. Every figure on this page is invented.

Illustrative data only

Scores are generated to show the shape of each view. The capability names are the real model (8 domains, 52 capabilities); the numbers attached to them are not an assessment and must not be quoted.

Each view below is built from the same two numbers the model carries: maturity (1–5, what the institution is able to do, evidence-gated) and readiness (0–5, how well the enterprise has packaged a way of doing it). They are never merged into one score. Most of these views exist precisely to show the distance between them.

The one they will ask for

Capability heat map

Every capability on one page, filled by how able we are, with how far the platform has already carried it drawn along the bottom edge of each tile.

Audience
Executive committee, EA board, any first conversation
Data it needs
A maturity rating per capability and the best readiness among its realizations
Before it is honest
Rubrics. Until a capability has one, its fill colour is an opinion — mark those tiles provisional

Read the mismatch, not the colour. A pale tile with a long orange edge is the finding worth presenting: the platform has packaged it, the institution still cannot do it to standard. A dark tile with no edge is the opposite — capable people, nothing reusable behind them.

The brief, answered directly

Coverage against ability

The same 52 capabilities counted twice: what is approved and available for general use today, and what we are actually able to do to the standard the institution needs.

Audience
Senior leadership — the “are we behind?” conversation
Data it needs
Readiness ≥ 2 (approved) on the left; maturity ≥ 3 on the right
Why two maps
One number would let the good news stand in for the work. Two counts side by side cannot be collapsed

This is the slide that answers “so we are not that bad”. The left map is genuinely good news and should be presented as such. The right map is the work. Putting them on one page in the same shape is what stops either from being quoted alone.

Diagnostic

Readiness against maturity

One dot per capability. The quadrant a dot lands in tells you what kind of intervention it needs — and they are four different kinds.

Audience
EA, platform owners, the AI CoE — a working session, not a board slide
Data it needs
Both scales, per capability. Nothing else
What it settles
Stops “buy more platform” being the answer to every gap

Bottom-right is the expensive quadrant. Something has been bought, standardised and industrialised, and the institution still cannot use it to the standard it needs. The fix is never more platform — it is enablement, ownership, or a rule nobody has written yet. Top-left is the mirror image: capable people carrying it by hand, with nothing reusable behind them.

Prioritisation

Gap to target

Where we are, where we said we need to be, and the distance between — by domain, largest gap first.

Audience
Portfolio and investment committee
Data it needs
Current maturity, and an agreed target per domain
Before it is honest
The target must be signed by someone. An unsigned target makes every gap arguable

Target is a decision, not a benchmark. Level 5 everywhere is neither achievable nor desirable; several capabilities are correctly finished at 3. The gap bar is only meaningful once leadership has agreed what “enough” means for each domain.

The single slide

Assessment profile

If only one slide survives the deck, this is it: four numbers and the distribution behind them.

Audience
Board, or any audience with four minutes
Data it needs
Counts only — derived from the two scales
Watch for
An average maturity is a comfortable number that hides every real finding. Show the distribution beside it or not at all

Never present the average alone. Two estates with the same mean look identical and behave nothing alike — one is uniformly adequate, the other has a cluster of strength and a cluster of exposure. The bars are the finding; the mean is the caption.

Shape at a glance

Domain scorecard

Eight domains, current against target, in the shape management already recognises from every other maturity assessment they have seen.

Audience
Executive, and any audience expecting a familiar format
Data it needs
Domain-level roll-up of maturity and target
Honest caveat
A radar exaggerates area and the axis order changes the shape. Use it to be recognised, then move to the bars for anything decided

Include it knowing what it is. The enclosed area has no meaning and reordering the axes changes the silhouette without changing a single number. It earns its place by being instantly readable to an audience that has seen twenty of them — not by being precise.

The answer to “so what?”

Roadmap horizons

The gap analysis turned into three waves, with each capability placed by the size of its gap and what has to exist before it can start.

Audience
Everyone. This is the deliverable the assessment exists to produce
Data it needs
Gap size, effort estimate, and the enabler edges between capabilities
Before it is honest
Sequencing must follow dependencies, not enthusiasm. A capability in wave one whose enabler sits in wave two is a plan that fails quietly

Wave one is deliberately small. A first wave that contains everything urgent is not a roadmap, it is a wish list. The constraint is delivery capacity and the dependency order — both of which the model already holds. In this mock the ordering is gap size alone; the real sequencing runs the enabler edges over it, which will move several of these.

Operating model

Accountability spread

Every capability grouped under the role accountable for it, stacked by maturity — which makes an unowned or over-loaded role impossible to miss.

Audience
The operating-model discussion, and anyone being asked to accept accountability
Data it needs
Proposed accountable role per capability
What it exposes
Roles that do not exist yet, roles carrying more than one person plausibly can, and accountability spread so thin it is nobody's

The tail is the finding, not the top of the list. A model that spreads 52 capabilities across forty-odd roles has not distributed accountability, it has diluted it — and most of those roles do not exist on anyone's job description yet. This view usually generates more discussion than the heat map, because it is the first time anyone is asked to put their name against a row.

For the second and third line

Control and evidence exposure

The governance domain on its own terms: what we claim, what evidence exists behind the claim, and what an auditor would find missing.

Audience
Cybersecurity, Risk, Legal, Data Governance, Internal Audit
Data it needs
Maturity, evidence coverage, and the obligations register mapped to capabilities
Before it is honest
ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A and NIST AI RMF are not yet mapped. Carve this domain out of any approval request until they are

Evidence coverage is the column that matters. A maturity rating without evidence behind it is a self-assessment, and the second line will treat it as one. This view is how the assessment survives contact with Internal Audit.

Reporting cadence

Trajectory

The same map at three points in time, so the assessment becomes a tracked commitment rather than a one-off exercise.

Audience
Quarterly governance reporting
Data it needs
The baseline, plus a re-rating on the same rubric at each checkpoint
Before it is honest
Same rubric, same evidence standard, every time. A re-rating against a softer bar is movement that did not happen

This view is what turns the assessment into an instrument. Run once it is a document; run quarterly against a fixed rubric it becomes the thing the roadmap is measured against.

Three more worth building, not mocked here

  • Dependency chain — the typed enabler edges already in the model, drawn as “X cannot progress until Y exists”. Useful in exactly one meeting: the one where someone asks why a capability is in wave three.
  • Agentic cut — the heat map filtered to the capabilities that only become necessary once agents act without a human in the loop. Answers “can we do agents?” without answering it with a product name.
  • Peer comparison — the same domains against comparable institutions. Wait for it. A benchmark against an unnamed peer set is the fastest way to lose an argument about scoring.
